CoreWeave

The neocloud that runs the hyperscaler business model without the hyperscaler balance sheet — and shows what that costs.

08 Cloud & Neoclouds16 Capital & Policy

CoreWeave is the cleanest natural experiment in the AI buildout. It does what Microsoft and Amazon do — buy accelerators, wrap them in power and networking, rent them out — with none of the software revenue that lets the hyperscalers absorb a bad year. Every cost the others can bury shows up on its income statement.

1.Role in the stack

A pure-play GPU cloud. It buys silicon on debt, sells capacity on multi-year contracts, and lives on the spread. That makes it the layer's price discovery mechanism: when reserved GPU pricing moves, CoreWeave's economics move with it, undisguised.

Revenue was $2.078bn in Q1 2026, up 112% [s], on a backlog of $99.4bn [s]. The company posted a 56% adjusted EBITDA margin and a $740m GAAP net loss in the same quarter [s]. Both are true, and the bridge between them is $536m of quarterly net interest [s] on $24.859bn of debt against $4.759bn of equity [s]. Capex of $7.695bn ran against operating cash flow of $2.984bn [s].

Concentration is the other exposure. Microsoft was 62% of 2024 revenue [s]; NVIDIA holds equity, having bought 22,935,780 Class A shares at $87.20 for $2bn in January 2026 [s]. Its supplier is also its shareholder, and its largest customers are the same firms building competing capacity.

3.The bull case

The contracts are signed. A $99.4bn backlog against $12–13bn of guided 2026 revenue [f] is years of visibility, and the counterparties are OpenAI at up to $11.9bn [s] and Meta at $21bn [s] — not speculative demand. A 56% EBITDA margin says the operating business works; the losses are a financing structure, and financing structures can be refinanced.

The pricing environment supports it. One-year reserved H100 contracts bottomed at $1.70/GPU-hour in October 2025 and rebounded about 38% to $2.35 by March 2026 [s], because providers sold out and little Hopper capacity came off contract. The oversupply story is too simple.

4.The bear case

Michael Burry's depreciation argument lands here hardest [o], because CoreWeave has nowhere to hide it. And the financing has become explicit about what is really being lent against: the $8.5bn delayed-draw facility closed on 30 March 2026 sits in a bankruptcy-remote subsidiary, secured on substantially all of that subsidiary's assets and a pledge of its equity [s]. It prices at SOFR plus 225 basis points with a 50 basis point undrawn fee, a 1.15x debt-service-coverage covenant and a 2032 maturity [s].

Read those terms together and they describe the risk precisely: a leveraged-credit spread, a fee that makes idle commitment expensive, a covenant that binds on cash flow rather than asset value, and a six-year tenor against hardware whose useful life the layer below cannot agree on — Amazon shortened server lives while Meta extended them in the same month [s].

There is also evidence the market will say no. Core Scientific's shareholders rejected CoreWeave's all-stock acquisition on 30 October 2025, with about 91% of votes cast against [s].

◆What to watch

Falsifiable indicators — each one resolves on a disclosure, not on an opinion.

  1. 01Whether GAAP net loss narrows as revenue scales, or whether interest expense grows with it. Q1 2026 was a $740m loss against $536m of quarterly net interest — if the second number keeps pace with revenue, the first will not close.
  2. 02The 1.15x debt-service-coverage covenant on the March 2026 facility. It binds on cash flow, not on asset value, which makes it the first thing a demand pause would touch.
  3. 03Customer concentration in the next 10-Q. Microsoft was 62% of 2024 revenue; the Meta agreement diversifies the name at the top without changing the shape.
  4. 04Whether any GPU-secured facility is refinanced rather than repaid. The March 2026 loan put the GPUs themselves in the lien, so a refinancing tells you what the collateral is actually worth.
