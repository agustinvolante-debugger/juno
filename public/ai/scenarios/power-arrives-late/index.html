scenario

Power arrives late

What if the machines are bought, the contracts are signed, and the electricity to run them is three years behind schedule?

01 Primary Energy→02 Grid & Interconnect→03 Datacenter Physical→08 Cloud & Neoclouds
Trigger
Load-queue studies, turbine deliveries and transmission builds slip past the in-service dates already written into contracted cloud capacity, so energisation rather than silicon sets the delivery date.
Tripwire
A hyperscaler or neocloud attributing a capacity or revenue shortfall to energisation delay on an earnings call or in a filing — or ERCOT's Batch Zero transmission plan, due fall 2027, pushing its cohort past 2030.
Standing
Material — the evidence leans this way

The queues are not forecasts. They are filings, and they are the least speculative evidence on this site.

1.The mechanism: the bottleneck inverted

Until about 2024 the interconnection problem was about getting generation onto the grid. It is now about getting load off it. ERCOT was tracking 438 GW of large-load interconnection requests, nearly 90% of it data centers [s], against an all-time peak demand of 85,508 MW [s] — roughly five times the largest hour the system has ever served. The queue got there fast: 63 GW at the end of 2024, 226 GW by November 2025 [s].

PJM reopened its queue in April 2026 and drew 811 projects totalling 220 GW of nameplate capacity [s], gas-fired generation leading at 106 GW [s]. Nameplate is the word that matters, and PJM says so itself. Only 24% of its 2020 entry cohort ever reached an interconnection agreement or became operational [s]. Generation interconnection in PJM averaged 40 months against a federal target of eight to eleven [e], and in the data-center load-growth zones of PJM and ERCOT, active projects were waiting 36 to 48 months [e].

2.Equipment and wires, separately

Turbines. GE Vernova had roughly 10 GW of combined 2029 and 2030 delivery capacity left after Q1 2026 [s] — one vendor, roughly a third of the global heavy-duty market, not an industry ceiling. Directional lead times on new heavy-duty orders were about 36 months [e], with industry waits reported as high as 84 months [e]. Global orders hit 110 GW in 2025 against manufacturing capacity of 60 to 70 GW a year [e], and BNP Paribas put heavy-duty pricing near $790 per kW by July 2026 [e]. Scarcity is showing up in price, which is the honest test of a shortage claim.

Wires are worse, and nobody is expanding them. The US built 888 miles of new 345 kV and above transmission in 2024 against roughly 5,000 miles a year indicated as necessary by the DOE's own planning study [s]. Capacity markets are already pricing the gap: PJM's 2028–2029 auction cleared at its $325 per MW-day cap [s], 6.8 GW below the installed reserve margin target, attracting just 525 MW of new supply [s]. A price cap and a shortfall in the same auction means the market cleared on administrative fiat, not on supply.

3.The path the shock takes

Primary energy to grid is where the delay originates and where it compounds — turbine slots and interconnection studies are sequential, not parallel.

Grid to datacenter is already observable. Power availability was named the most prominent obstacle to on-schedule delivery by 48% of more than 280 surveyed industry experts [s]. Local politics adds to it: Data Center Watch counted 75 US projects worth about $130bn blocked or delayed in Q1 2026 [e], a count from an advocacy-adjacent tracker with no published method, which is why it is an estimate here.

Datacenter to cloud is where it becomes financial. Contracted backlog carries delivery dates. Microsoft holds $633bn of remaining performance obligations and says demand exceeds supply [s]; Alphabet's RPO is $519.5bn [s]; CoreWeave's backlog is $99.4bn [s]. Late energisation does not cancel that revenue, it defers it — while the machines bought to serve it depreciate on schedule. Nebius guided $20–25bn of 2026 capex against $3.0–3.4bn of revenue [f]; that ratio only works if the capacity energises roughly when planned.

4.Who says this will not happen

The steelman here is stronger than for any other scenario on this site, and it comes from inside the industry.

Scott Strazik, GE Vernova's CEO, says the turbines are "really not the gating item," pointing instead at EPC buildout, permitting and fuel availability — said on the same call that produced the 36-month lead time [e]. Ed Crooks of Wood Mackenzie says GE Vernova, Siemens Energy and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries have all announced significant gas-equipment capacity expansions since the start of 2025 [o]. Wood Mackenzie projects 63 GW of turbine manufacturing capacity additions between 2026 and 2030 and expects orders to peak in 2026 [f]; GE Vernova targets 30 GW of annual output by 2030 [f].

Grid Strategies supplies the demand-side counter: the data-center portion of utility load forecasts is likely overstated by roughly 25 GW [e]. The same report's other half must travel with it — 2022–2025 load forecasts were revised up, not down. Meanwhile faster supply exists: EIA projects US solar capacity rising from 151 GW in 2025 to 223 GW in 2027 [f], fuel is cheap at a forecast $3.67 per MMBtu Henry Hub [f], and buyers are simply paying to bypass the queue — Microsoft's 2 GW Pecos site is being built with the company funding the energy infrastructure [s].

5.What would falsify this

  • PJM's Cycle 1 realisation rate runs well above the 24% precedent [s]. If most of the 220 GW nameplate [s] reaches an interconnection agreement, the queue is a pipeline rather than a waiting room.
  • GE Vernova hits its output ramp on schedule and 2029 slots reopen. Published slot availability moving out rather than in kills the equipment leg.
  • 345 kV-plus mileage climbs toward the DOE benchmark. This is annual, published, and currently off by roughly a factor of five.
  • A PJM capacity auction clears below its cap with meaningful new supply. Two consecutive uncapped clears with new entry would say the market solved it.
