Inference & Serving

Token economics, batching, KV cache, price deflation

This is the layer that is supposed to be a commodity and keeps refusing to behave like one.

The deflation is the fastest measured anywhere in the stack: across six benchmarks, the price of a fixed level of capability fell at a median 50x per year [e]. And in July 2026 Anthropic scheduled a price increase — Sonnet 5 output goes from $10 to $15 per million tokens on 1 September 2026 [s]. Both facts are true, which means the simple story about token prices is wrong.

1.What this layer is

Training produces a file. Inference is the business of turning that file into tokens on demand, profitably, for many users at once.

The physical anchor is memory, not arithmetic. Each in-flight conversation holds an attention cache on the accelerator; serving well means packing as many of those as possible into one pass of the same weights. Batching, KV-cache management, quantization and splitting prefill from decode are the whole craft, and they are what stands between a GPU-hour you rent and a token you sell.

Four kinds of company sell here. Model owners serve their own weights through their own APIs. Managed platforms — Fireworks, Together, Baseten — serve anyone's weights, mostly open ones, on rented or owned fleets. Routers sit above everything and sell placement. Silicon-backed servers build custom hardware and sell only its output.

Two units run this layer, and the spread between them is the business. Upstream you buy GPU-hours: Together's on-demand H100 was $3.99 per GPU-hour in July 2026 [s]. Downstream you sell tokens.

And identical silicon does not rent at one price. Published H100 on-demand pricing across Modal, Baseten, Fireworks and Together spanned $3.95 to $7.00 per GPU-hour in July 2026, a spread of about 1.8 times [s] — four first-party price lists, same hardware, fetched the same day. Hold that number. It is the cleanest evidence on this page that serving is not yet a commodity, and it is the only such evidence that does not depend on an analyst's estimate.

On the token side, open weights price at near-parity across independent providers, which is what commoditization looks like when it arrives. Groq serves gpt-oss-120B at $0.15 per million input and $0.60 per million output tokens at approximately 500 tokens per second [s]. Frontier weights price roughly forty times higher: Claude Opus 5 at $5 input and $25 output per million [s], OpenAI's gpt-5.6-sol at $5 and $30 [s]. Cached-input and batch tiers cut both further; the discount structures are near-identical across the two vendors, which is itself a commoditization signal at the frontier tier.

Margins are the other half of the argument, and there the evidence is thin. Fireworks' gross margin was approximately 50 percent as of mid-2026, against a stated target of 60 percent [e]. Sacra estimates Together's at approximately 45 percent [e]. Read those two citations together: both legs of the "managed serving holds margin" case are the same analyst, and neither figure appears in a funding announcement, a company statement, or any independent coverage. The price dispersion above is T1 and first-party. The margins are one shop's estimates, and this page weights them accordingly.

forecast · Fireworks AI, relayed by SacraFireworks targets 60 percent gross margin [e], up from the ~50 percent estimated at mid-2026. A managed platform hitting that on rented accelerators would be the strongest single argument that serving is not a commodity — and it would also be the first such figure anyone had confirmed from outside the company.

3.The players

Fireworks surpassed $1bn in annualized revenue run-rate in July 2026 [s] — company-stated, and a multiple of where it sat at the end of 2025. That supersedes the $800m Sacra estimated for May 2026 [e], which should now be read as a ten-week-old waypoint and never as a current figure; the difference resets the company's revenue multiple materially.

Together AI is estimated at $1.0bn annualized as of February 2026 [e]. The independently corroborated figures for Together are its Series C and its bookings, and bookings are not revenue; the revenue estimate itself is single-source.

Baseten reached an estimated $600m annualized in March 2026, up from $200m in December 2025 [e]. An apparent inconsistency between that step and the company's own year-over-year growth claim resolves as a misreading — they measure different intervals, not different numbers.

OpenRouter processed about 100 trillion tokens per month across more than 8 million developers as of May 2026 [s]. Earlier volume figures still in circulation — including the one this corpus itself held until July 2026 — are more than an order of magnitude stale, and pair a 2026 date with a developer count from a year earlier. A router that sees a hundred trillion tokens a month knows more about real model demand than anyone selling models.

Groq is the cautionary tale. It signed a non-exclusive licensing agreement with Nvidia on 24 December 2025 worth $17bn in cash across three installments [s], distributed most of it to shareholders, and sent its CEO and president to Nvidia while promoting its CFO. It reads as a soft exit of the independent-accelerator thesis, even as the remaining company raised $650m in June 2026 to expand its inference cloud [s].

4.Concentration & chokepoints

There are two chokepoints and they sit at opposite ends.

Below, the silicon is effectively one vendor, and the most credible independent challenger licensed its position to that vendor for $17bn [s]. Serving-cost improvements therefore arrive on someone else's roadmap.

Above, demand discovery is concentrating into routers. At 100 trillion tokens a month [s], OpenRouter is a distribution chokepoint for every provider that does not own an end-user relationship — and it takes a percentage of routed spend for the service.

What is not concentrated is serving know-how. vLLM reported serving DeepSeek at 2,200 output tokens per second per H200 GPU using wide expert parallelism and async scheduling [s], published openly, alongside public work on FP8 KV-cache quantization and prefill-decode disaggregation. The efficiency frontier here diffuses through open-source blog posts within weeks. That is excellent for buyers and corrosive for anyone whose margin depends on a serving trick.

5.Lead times & capacity

This layer has no build queue of its own. It inherits one: GPU availability, power and shells, all upstream. What it controls is throughput per unit of rented capacity, and that is improving fast enough to be a substitute for capacity.

Demand-side efficiency moves too, and harder than most forecasts allow. Anthropic's code-execution-with-MCP example cut agent token consumption from 150,000 to 2,000 tokens, a 98.7 percent reduction [s]. A single architectural change at the application layer can remove two orders of magnitude of demand from this one. Token-volume projections that extrapolate from current agent designs are extrapolating from a design that is actively being optimised away.

6.2019 → today

2019. Inference is an afterthought — a model runs on the machine that trained it, and serving cost is a footnote in a research budget.

2022–24. Serving becomes an industry with a price list. Frontier reasoning output sells at $60 per million tokens as late as January 2025 [s], and the deflation rate accelerates: restricted to data after January 2024, the median price decline rises to 200x per year [e].

2026. Three prices coexist that would have looked incoherent two years earlier. Open weights at $0.60 per million output tokens [s]. Frontier weights at $25 [s] to $30 [s]. And a scheduled 50 percent list increase on a mid-tier model [s].

7.Commoditization pressure

The case for commoditization is strong and quantitative. A median 50x annual price decline per unit of capability [e], rising to 200x when the data is restricted to after January 2024 [e]. Near-parity pricing on identical open weights across independent providers [s]. Serving optimizations published in public repositories [s]. On the evidence a buyer sees, this looks like electricity.

The case against has four parts, of very unequal strength. The strongest is price dispersion: a 1.8x spread on identical H100s across four providers' own published rate cards [s]. Commodities do not price 80 percent apart on the same day for the same hardware. Second, a hard fact — a vendor with pricing power raised list price 50 percent [s], ending introductory pricing rather than defending share. Third, and nearly invisible in price tables: a tokenizer that emits more tokens for the same text raises the effective price per request without changing a single number in the published rate card. Fourth and weakest, though the most quoted: gross margins of approximately 50 percent [e] and approximately 45 percent [e] at the two largest managed platforms — one analyst, twice.

So the honest reading is that the token is commoditizing and the platform is not, yet — and that the durable evidence for the second half is dispersion in first-party prices, not the margin estimates everyone cites. A single audited margin disclosure would settle the most important question on this page.

8.Failure modes

Margin inversion on agentic workloads. Replit's gross margin fluctuated between positive 36 percent and negative 14 percent during 2025 [e]. That is the clearest public evidence that per-token COGS can invert a downstream business's economics — and it rests on a single unsourced sentence with no independent corroboration, which is itself the finding.

Router dependency. Providers that reach developers through a router do not own the demand. Placement and take rate are set by someone else.

Capacity mismatch. Reserved GPU commitments are long; token prices fall 50x a year per unit of capability [e]. Contract the wrong duration at the wrong price and the deflation lands on your gross margin rather than your customer's bill.

Demand-side collapse. A 98.7 percent token reduction from one agent-architecture change [s] shows how fast volume assumptions can break in the buyer's favour.

Estimate risk. Nearly every revenue and margin figure for the private companies in this layer is a Sacra estimate. If those estimates are systematically high, the layer's growth story and its margin story are both weaker than they appear, and nothing public would reveal it.

9.Open questions

  • What are managed-serving gross margins really? Approximately 50 percent at Fireworks [e] and approximately 45 percent at Together [e] come from one analyst, and both are typed as estimates. Neither company has confirmed a margin. These remain the corpus's most load-bearing unverified numbers, and the layer's verdict leans on first-party price dispersion instead [s] precisely because of that.
  • Is the Sonnet price increase an exception or a turn? [s] One scheduled rise inside a 50x-a-year deflation [e] could be the end of introductory pricing or the start of frontier pricing power. Nothing here distinguishes them.
  • Where does the deflation actually stop? 50x a year is a median across six benchmarks [e], and the per-benchmark rates spread widely around it — the corpus holds that range only in a source note. A median drawn from a wide spread is a description of the past, not a forecast.
  • Does specialisation survive open-weight parity? Fireworks attributes most of its volume to customer-specialised models. If specialisation is real differentiation, parity pricing on stock open weights is beside the point; if it is fine-tuning as a service, it is a feature, not a moat.
  • Who captures the router's economics at scale? OpenRouter sees 100 trillion tokens a month [s] on a take rate the corpus records only in a source note. The margin structure of the layer's distribution chokepoint is not documented.
