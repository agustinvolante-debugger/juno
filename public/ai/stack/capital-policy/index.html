Capital & Policy

Circular deals, private credit, export controls, sovereign AI

Two documents decide whether the other fifteen layers get built. A term sheet, and an export licence.

This is the layer where the discourse is loudest and the underlying paper is softest. The two most-quoted numbers in the entire AI buildout — NVIDIA's "$100bn into OpenAI" [s] and OpenAI's "$1.4 trillion of commitments" [o] — are, respectively, a letter of intent that had not closed as of NVIDIA's own annual filing, and a figure Sam Altman attributed in November 2025 covering roughly eight years. Meanwhile the paper that is binding — an $8.5bn term loan secured on substantially all the assets of a bankruptcy-remote CoreWeave subsidiary and a pledge of its equity [s] — gets almost no coverage at all. This page is organised around that inversion.

August 2026 supplied a third, larger example. NVIDIA signed memorandums of understanding with Apollo Global, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilise more than $500bn of third-party capital for the build-out [s]. Read the instrument, not the headline: memorandums of understanding, and NVIDIA's own language is that the deal is subject to final agreement. It belongs in the same column as the letter of intent and the attributed total — a number that shapes expectations without yet obliging anyone to pay.

The market said as much on the day. NVIDIA fell about 1.1% on the report and closed 2.9% lower, erasing close to $60bn of market capitalisation [s]. A half-trillion-dollar funding partnership announced by a supplier read to investors as evidence of circularity rather than of demand, which is the cleanest market verdict on this layer's structure anyone has yet delivered.

Two more figures arrived with it, and both are forecasts rather than measurements: Morgan Stanley's projection that hyperscalers spend $3.5tn between 2026 and 2028 [f], and Apollo president Jim Zelter's estimate of more than $8tn of eventual investment [f] — the latter from an executive whose firm earns fees financing it. The same reporting carries one item that is not a number at all: NVIDIA in talks to guarantee a 10 GW data centre in Ohio leased to OpenAI, sourced to one person familiar with the matter, with no amount and no conclusion [s]. Recorded here at low confidence precisely because it is the shape of claim this layer keeps mistaking for a commitment.

1.What this layer is

Money that must be repaid on a schedule, and permission that can be withdrawn by rule.

The capital half has three tiers. Investment-grade bonds issued on a hyperscaler's own credit. Private credit and asset-backed structures, where the collateral is a rack of GPUs or a lease on a building. Vendor equity, where the supplier of the chips is also an investor in the buyer of the chips.

The policy half is one agency's rulebook. The Bureau of Industry and Security decides which accelerators may leave the United States, for whom, and on what conditions — and its rulebook has been written, rescinded, and in one central case left unreplaced.

The physical anchor: a signature page, and a certification form. Neither appears in any of the fifteen layers below, and both can stop all of them.

The price of AI money is set by where the asset sits, not by what the asset is — and CoreWeave's $8.5bn delayed-draw term loan, closed 30 March 2026, is the cleanest demonstration. It runs through a bankruptcy-remote subsidiary, is secured by substantially all of that subsidiary's assets plus a pledge of its equity, and funds GPU capital expenditure for an unnamed customer contract [s]. That structure is the correction that matters: the corpus previously recorded the collateral as having shifted from GPUs to customer contracts — lending against counterparty credit rather than asset value. The filed structure is the opposite. The GPUs are the lien. The customer contract sits in the covenant package, not in the security.

The terms are the part worth memorising, because they are the only actual price of AI credit in this corpus rather than a headline about one. The facility priced at SOFR plus 225 basis points, with a 50 basis point fee on the undrawn commitment, a 1.15 times debt-service-coverage covenant, and a maturity of 31 March 2032 [s]. Read those four numbers together and the structure explains itself: a spread that prices this as leveraged credit rather than infrastructure, a fee that makes idle capacity expensive, a coverage test that binds on cash flow rather than on asset value, and a six-year tenor set against GPUs whose useful life the operators one layer down cannot agree on.

At the other end of the spectrum, the same class of asset gets financed at investment grade by moving it off the sponsor's balance sheet entirely. Meta and Blue Owl issued approximately $27bn of A+ rated bonds plus about $2.5bn of equity through a joint-venture SPV arranged by Morgan Stanley in October 2025, anchored by PIMCO [s]. Same electrons, same silicon, a rating gap of many notches — and the gap is structural rather than operational. Compare the unsecured route on a hyperscaler's own credit: Meta's $30bn October 2025 bond drew an order book of about $125bn [s], roughly four times the deal, and Oracle raised $18bn in September 2025 largely to serve one customer contract [s].

The asset class did not exist three years ago. CoreWeave's $2.3bn facility of August 2023, led by Magnetar and Blackstone and collateralized by H100s [s], was the first at-scale GPU-backed loan; the $7.5bn delayed-draw facility followed in May 2024 [s].

3.The loop

In a normal capital structure the supplier, the customer and the investor are three parties. Here they are frequently one or two, and NVIDIA is the clearest case.

NVIDIA announced a letter of intent in September 2025 to invest up to $100bn in OpenAI, released progressively as each gigawatt of at least 10 GW of NVIDIA systems is deployed [s]. Three qualifiers travel with that number and the site will not print it without them. "Letter of intent" is NVIDIA's own word in its own newsroom release, not a paraphrase — an earlier correction wrongly recast it as a memorandum of understanding on a Wikipedia citation, inverting the source hierarchy. NVIDIA's own forward-looking-statements section flags the $100bn as forward-looking, and the release closes by looking forward to "finalizing the details in the coming weeks." And NVIDIA's FY2026 10-K, filed months later, confirms no transaction had completed.

The same pattern repeats across the layer, each instance individually defensible: $5bn into Intel [s]; a pre-funded warrant for 21,065,936 Nebius shares raising about $2bn [s]; a $2bn private placement of CoreWeave Class A shares at $87.20 [s]; an agreement to purchase CoreWeave's residual unsold capacity valued at $6.3bn through 2032 [s]; and, with Microsoft, up to $15bn combined into Anthropic [s] in the same month Anthropic committed to purchase $30bn of Azure capacity running on NVIDIA systems [s].

AMD's version is the most explicit price ever put on demand. It issued OpenAI a warrant on 5 October 2025 for up to 160 million shares at $0.01 each, vesting in tranches tied to Instinct deployments with the final tranche gated on a $600 share price [s]. The load-bearing term is not the share count: full vesting is contingent on 6 GW of deployment [s]. The "about 10% of the company" line in circulation appears nowhere in the 8-K — it is press arithmetic on the share count.

Microsoft holds both sides at once: an investment in OpenAI Group PBC valued at approximately $135bn, about 27% on an as-converted diluted basis after the October 2025 recapitalization [s], while OpenAI simultaneously contracted to buy an incremental $250bn of Azure services [s]. Oracle's $300bn five-year, 4.5 GW contract with OpenAI [s] is the reason its remaining performance obligations reached $455bn in Q1 FY2026, up 359% year over year [s]. AWS added $38bn [s], and CoreWeave's combined OpenAI contracts reached about $22.4bn [s].

The one genuinely new thing in the loop is a buyer from outside it: Jane Street signed a $6bn AI cloud agreement with CoreWeave and made a $1bn equity investment [s]. A gigawatt-scale compute buyer that is neither a hyperscaler nor a lab is the only datapoint here that widens the counterparty base rather than tightening it.

4.How to read $1.4 trillion

This is the most abused number in the AI discourse, and getting it right is the single most useful thing this page does.

Sam Altman said in November 2025 that OpenAI had about $1.4tn of data-center commitments, covering roughly 30 GW over about eight years [o]. Four things follow.

It is an attributed figure, not a sum. The corpus previously recorded it as an aggregation of the Oracle, Microsoft, AWS and chip deals. That method was defective three ways: the cited contracts total $588bn, not $1,400bn, so roughly $800bn was unverifiable under the site's own rules; it counted NVIDIA's up-to-$100bn investment into OpenAI as an outbound purchase; and Oracle's $300bn is largely delivered through Stargate sites carrying their own $500bn headline, so the sum double-counted. Any chart presenting this as a stacked bar of the named deals is wrong. The site's own cited contracts account for $588bn of it ; the balance is not publicly itemised.

The denominator has to be named. OpenAI's annualized revenue run rate exceeded $20bn exiting 2025, against full-year 2025 revenue of approximately $13bn [e]. The comparison in circulation uses the $13bn, which is a full-year figure and roughly 35% low against the metric the sentence claims to be measuring. Use the run rate.

The horizon has to be stated. Commitments spanning about eight years set against a single year of revenue compare unlike things. The valid observation is narrower and still striking: a company whose equity was marked at $500bn in an October 2025 secondary sale, up from $157bn a year earlier [s], has signed forward compute obligations that are a large multiple of any revenue it has yet earned.

Stargate's own headline is a plan, not a contract. It was announced in January 2025 with a stated intent to spend up to $500bn on US AI infrastructure by 2029 [f] — logged as a company claim. The reported equity structure was never confirmed by the parties and does not internally reconcile: SoftBank and OpenAI at $19bn each for 40%, with Oracle and MGX at about $7bn each [e] — components that do not reconcile with other researchers' descriptions of founding-partner equity in the same vehicle.

5.Where the leverage actually sits

The vehicle is the point. Private capital groups including Apollo and Blackstone have built multibillion-dollar, investment-grade-rated financings for companies such as Meta and Intel that sit off those companies' corporate balance sheets [s]. Capital is raised into a separately rated entity, so the obligation never appears as corporate debt and never shows up in the leverage ratios anyone screens on. That is the same architecture as the bankruptcy-remote subsidiary below, sold to a different buyer — and it is why a sector that looks under-levered at the parent can be carrying a great deal of contracted obligation one entity down.

Not on OpenAI's balance sheet. This is the correction that matters most in the layer, because the entire circular-capital argument is about whose credit carries the risk.

A JPMorgan-led lender group provided a $7.1bn construction loan in May 2025 — and the borrower is the Crusoe–Blue Owl joint venture developing Abilene Phase 2, which Oracle leases, not OpenAI [s]. The corpus previously named OpenAI as borrower and conflated this facility with a smaller Phase 1 loan from late 2024. SoftBank's $15bn one-year bridge, signed 10 April 2025 across 21 banks, is real and closed [s].

The template is documented in Meta's own filings, and it is worth reading carefully because it will be copied. Meta and Blue Owl formed a joint venture in October 2025 to develop the Richland Parish campus, committing to fund pro rata shares of approximately $27bn in total estimated development costs [s] — that is a cost estimate funded over time, not the JV's size. Now put the headline next to what was actually capitalised. At formation Meta contributed $4.3bn and Blue Owl $7.0bn in cash — $11.3bn in total — and Meta immediately took a one-time distribution of $2.6bn while holding a 20% interest [s]. So the venture that carries a $27bn number opened with $11.3bn of equity, of which Meta's net cash outlay was $1.7bn . That gap is not an accounting curiosity — it is the structure. Meta then leases the campus back, with operating leases commencing at the end of the decade, renewal options extending for decades after that, and a residual value guarantee covering the venture's early years. The Venture is an unconsolidated variable interest entity accounted for under the equity method — which is the whole point of the structure.

The number that actually measures the exposure is not the $27bn headline. Meta disclosed maximum exposure to loss of $45.95bn against an equity carrying value of $1.83bn as of 31 December 2025 [s]. Twenty-five times the carried equity, on one venture, disclosed by the sponsor, with no residual-value-guarantee liability recorded.

6.2019 → today

2019. AI capex comes out of operating cash flow. Export controls are entity-specific and rare. There is no such thing as a GPU-backed loan.

2023–24. The asset class is invented, and it is invented by one company: a $2.3bn GPU-collateralized facility [s] then a $7.5bn delayed-draw term loan [s]. Nobody had previously lent billions against depreciating silicon.

2025–26. Structure replaces balance sheet, and the regulator notices. $27bn of A+ JV bonds [s], an $8.5bn bankruptcy-remote facility [s], and concentration to match: the Magnificent 7 went from about 20% to nearly 35% of S&P 500 market capitalization between November 2022 and late 2025 [s]. 30% of the Federal Reserve's market-outreach contacts named AI among salient financial-stability risks in Fall 2025, up from 9% in Spring [s].

Michael Burry's claim that hyperscalers will understate depreciation by about $176bn across 2026–28 by extending server useful lives [o] belongs in this timeline as what it is: the opinion of a named short-seller with positions, arguing about the layer above.

7.What the export rules actually say

Read the rule text, not the preamble. The January 2026 BIS rule permits case-by-case licensing to China and Macau only for commodities certified below 21,000 total processing performance [s], with a companion cap on total DRAM bandwidth below 6,500 GB/s [s]; the rule names the H200 and MI325X as the reference class. Everything above the thresholds keeps a presumption of denial. The loosening covers a sub-frontier tier only.

Two details are routinely reported wrong. First, the volume cap is not a unit ratio. The rule caps licensed China and Macau exports at 50% of the aggregate total processing performance cumulatively shipped to US end users for the same commodity [s], measured from first commercial US shipment through to the licence application date. The preamble states it loosely in product-volume terms; the operative section binds on cumulative aggregate TPP, which is materially stricter — a vendor cannot backload China volume, and the ratio is scored against lifetime US performance shipped. Any chart showing this as a unit ratio is mislabelled.

Second, there are nine certification conditions, not five [s]: US volumes and specs, US supply sufficiency and no foundry diversion, the aggregate-TPP cap, no military or WMD end use, consignee KYC against remote access, a list of IaaS remote end users in named restricted destinations, a commitment not to transfer model weights or grant remote access to restricted parties, a physical-security demonstration, and third-party lab review of every shipment before export. The earlier count of five came from merging preamble bullets and dropping four conditions.

The Gulf side of the same policy is narrower than its headline too. BIS moved the UAE from Country Groups D:3 and D:4 to A:5 effective 10 July 2026, granting licence-free access to advanced computing items [s] — but the named approved entities in Supplement 8 are two: G42, including G42 Cloud Technology LLC, and Core42. MGX is not one of them; BIS says only that it intends to review MGX applications favourably, and licences are still required. And the authorisation for G42 and Core42 automatically expires on 6 April 2027 absent further notice. Supplement 8 separately lists eight US-headquartered AI companies and their subsidiaries, which makes the rule a US-cloud-in-the-Gulf authorisation as much as a UAE one. The frequently drawn contrast — the UAE gets no cap while China gets 50% — survives only with that sunset attached.

Two things the rulebook does not contain are as important as what it does. The GAIN AI Act passed the Senate inside the FY2026 NDAA on 9 October 2025 and was stripped before the Act was signed on 18 December [s], leaving export-control authority over AI chips discretionary and unlegislated. And the AI Diffusion rule — the first US control on model weights — was rescinded, with NVIDIA reporting in May 2026 that the scope, timing and requirements of any replacement remained uncertain 12 months later [s]. A Federal Register search returns neither a rescission rule nor a replacement.

Then there is the arrangement that exists only verbally. US officials expressed an expectation of receiving 15% or more of revenue from licensed NVIDIA sales to China — and NVIDIA's own 10-Q states that the government "did not publish a regulation codifying such requirement" [s]. A sovereign revenue share operating on expectation, absent from the January 2026 rule text.

8.The enforcement gap

Rules tightened on paper while the machinery that enforces them slowed down. Between 2018 and 2024, BIS added entities to the Entity List every 35 days on average [s]. As of 26 June 2026, eight months had passed with no additions at all — the longest gap since 2008 [s]. The September 2025 affiliates rule, which extended controls to any entity at least half-owned by a listed party and covered an estimated 20,000 companies in China [e], was suspended for 12 months and is reimposed effective 10 November 2026 [s].

Scale the enforcer against the problem. Gregory Allen reported BIS operating on a budget just under $200m with fewer than 600 employees [s] — a 2024 figure, with no newer public equivalent. Against that: a March 2026 indictment alleges a Southeast Asian intermediary purchased approximately $2.5bn of servers from a US manufacturer for unlicensed diversion to China [s], including at least about $510m in the three weeks between late April and mid-May 2025 alone [s]. The concealment method is the detail worth keeping: thousands of non-working physical replica servers staged to defeat the manufacturer's own compliance audits. That is the strongest available evidence that OEM end-use verification fails at scale. These are allegations, not adjudicated facts.

One case is adjudicated. Prosecutors stated that a Texas network exported or attempted to export at least $160m of controlled H100 and H200 GPUs between October 2024 and May 2025 [s] — and the principal pleaded guilty, which makes this the layer's one conviction rather than an allegation. A separate November 2025 indictment charges four defendants with routing NVIDIA GPUs to China through Malaysia and Thailand using a Florida realty company that had never conducted a real estate transaction [s], naming the transshipment corridor.

9.The other side of the wall

The most counter-intuitive finding in this layer: NVIDIA's China data-center collapse was caused by Beijing, not Washington. Chinese guidance in November 2025 requires state-funded data-centre projects to use only domestically made AI chips, with projects less than 30% complete ordered to remove installed foreign chips [s]. Two readings differ on whether the rule bans acquiring or also mandates removal, and NVIDIA's own filing does not describe it — its adjacent language is that the Chinese government "has encouraged customers to purchase from our China-based competitors," which is circumstantial support only. The result is not ambiguous: NVIDIA shipped no Data Center Hopper products to China in the quarter ended 26 April 2026, against $4.6bn a year earlier [s], and Jensen Huang said in April 2026 that NVIDIA's share of China's AI accelerator market had fallen to zero [o]. A country that believed itself choked would not ban the chips it can still get.

forecast · Bloomberg IntelligenceThe four largest US hyperscalers spend more than $400bn on AI capital expenditure in 2026, while five named Chinese cloud providers stay flat from a 2025 base below $40bn [f]. The Chinese side of that ratio counts five listed commercial cloud providers, not state-directed programmes, so read it as a comparison of corporate buildouts rather than of countries.

China's leverage is downstream instead. The IEA estimates full implementation of its expanded rare-earth export controls would put about $6.5tn a year of downstream production outside China at risk [f]. ASML records that China suspended those restrictions for 12 months until 10 November 2026 [s] — the same date the US affiliates rule comes back into force [s]. Two truces, negotiated separately, lapsing on one day. That is the calendar risk on this page.

The tail risk is Taiwan, and the honest position is that nobody has modelled it for the AI era. Rhodium Group estimated that a Chinese blockade halting all Taiwan trade would put well over $2tn of global economic activity at risk [f], and Rhodium itself calls that "a conservative and partial estimate," excluding second-order effects, escalation and sanctions. Bloomberg Economics' widely quoted alternative measures a different thing — first-year GDP loss, not activity at risk — so the two must not be averaged. Neither uses 2025 AI-era trade weights. That is a genuine gap, not a disagreement.

10.Sovereign AI: announced is not committed

The single most repeated error in this layer is treating an announcement as an appropriation. The corpus now separates them, and the results are unflattering.

The EU. The Commission has announced that €20bn will be mobilised for AI gigafactories through the InvestAI Facility, with the formal tender due summer 2026 and first construction in 2027 [f]. It was announced on 9 February 2025. Eighteen months later the tender has not issued, no site is funded, and nothing is under construction — which is why "mobilised" and not "committed" is the correct verb. The much larger figure widely attached to this programme is an EU-wide mobilisation target and does not appear on the Commission's own gigafactory page at all.

Japan. METI and NEDO awarded the five-year FRONTia foundation-model project, running fiscal 2026 through fiscal 2030, to Noetra Corp. and AIST on 30 June 2026 — and contracted only fiscal 2026 and 2027, with an annual stage-gate review deciding continuation thereafter [s]. There is no government-published total. The trillion-yen five-year figure and the first-year tranche that circulate with this programme both trace to a single news outlet; neither appears in any METI or NEDO document, and NEDO's own public call leaves the five-year total for the applicant to propose. This site therefore prints no Japanese sovereign AI number at all, which is itself the finding: the most-quoted sovereign AI commitment in Asia has no primary source behind its headline.

India. India's IndiaAI Mission was sanctioned in March 2024 at Rs 10,371.92 crore over five years, of which Rs 4,563.36 crore is the compute pillar [s]. Sanctioned is the operative word. Per the government's own parliamentary reply, roughly Rs 401 crore — about 3.9% of the sanctioned outlay — had actually reached the mission in its first two years [s], against successively larger revised estimates each year. No official US dollar equivalent exists for any of these figures, so the site does not print one.

South Korea. South Korea allocated KRW 10.1tn, about $7.25bn, to AI in its 2026 budget, more than tripling the KRW 3.3tn allocated for 2025 [s]. Proposed in August 2025 and cleared by the National Assembly, this is the one genuinely appropriated sovereign AI figure in the corpus, as distinct from announced, mobilised or sanctioned.

Set any of them against the private sector: Bridgewater expects about $650bn from four US companies in 2026 alone [f]. Sovereign AI, as currently funded, is an access-subsidy programme with a national-champion label. It is not a counterweight.

11.Failure modes

An intent that does not convert. NVIDIA's $100bn is staged per gigawatt and had not closed as of its own 10-K [s]; AMD's warrant only fully vests at 6 GW and a $600 share price [s]. If deployment slips, the equity that made the demand look funded never arrives, and the purchase commitments remain.

The structure that hides the exposure. $45.95bn of disclosed maximum loss against $1.83bn of carried equity [s] is one venture at one sponsor. Morgan Stanley's $800bn private-credit share of the financing need [f] is the part of this market with the least public disclosure, and therefore where a shock would be hardest to see.

A covenant, not a default. CoreWeave's $8.5bn facility lives inside a bankruptcy-remote subsidiary with a parent guarantee limited to specified bad acts [s], and the binding test is a 1.15 times debt-service-coverage covenant [s] — a ratio, not a payment. The first failure in this layer will look like a covenant breach at a subsidiary nobody has heard of, not a missed coupon at a parent everybody has.

Two truces lapsing together. 10 November 2026 [s][s]. Rare-earth restrictions return and the affiliates rule returns on the same day, in a market where enforcement staffing is a fraction of the flows it polices [s].

Policy by discretion. With the GAIN AI Act stripped [s], no replacement for the AI Diffusion rule [s], and a 15% revenue expectation that was never codified [s], the rules governing the industry's most valuable export can change without legislation. That is a risk to capital formation regardless of which direction they move.

12.Open questions

  • What is the other $800bn? Altman's $1.4tn [o] exceeds the site's cited contracts by roughly that much, and the balance is not publicly itemised. Until it is, no honest chart of OpenAI's commitments can be a stacked sum.
  • Does the NVIDIA–OpenAI letter of intent convert? NVIDIA's own filing says there is no assurance [s]. First phase was targeted at H2 2026 on Vera Rubin, which makes this falsifiable within months.
  • Who holds the residual on the leaseback? Meta discloses an exposure ceiling and a residual value guarantee on the leased campus [s], but ceilings are not probabilities and no liability had been recorded at year end [s].
  • Is the Entity List freeze policy or capacity? Eight months without an addition against a 35-day historical cadence [s][s] is consistent with a negotiated pause and equally consistent with an under-resourced agency. The corpus cannot distinguish them.
  • Does the under-levered-corporate steelman hold? Total US private nonfinancial credit was $42,235bn in Q2 2025 with debt-to-GDP at a two-decade low [s]. That is an aggregate. It says nothing about the leverage concentrated in the specific SPVs financing this buildout.
