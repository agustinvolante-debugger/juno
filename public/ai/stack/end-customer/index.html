layer 15 of 16 · field guide

End Customer

Enterprise budgets, ROI evidence, willingness to pay, churn

Every number in the fifteen layers below this one is a bet on this one. And this is the layer with the worst evidence in the stack.

There are no filings here. Enterprise AI demand is measured by surveys — run, in the two most-cited cases, by venture firms with positions in the answer — and by one payments processor's transaction data. The layer's headline finding and its headline counter-finding disagree by roughly a factor of nine on what looks like the same metric. That disagreement is the most useful thing on this page.

1.What this layer is

The buyer. A CIO with a budget line, a procurement process, and a security review.

The physical anchor is a purchase order and a renewal date. Enterprises spent $37bn on generative AI in 2025, up from $11.5bn in 2024 [e] — Menlo Ventures' market model plus a survey of 495 US enterprise AI decision-makers fielded in November 2025. It is verbatim-confirmed on Menlo's own page and has never been independently replicated, so it does not get upgraded past a medium-confidence estimate, and Menlo's investor incentive travels with it.

Set that against the layer directly upstream: combined 2025 capital expenditure across Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta was $376.1bn in cash [s]. The terminal customer's entire generative-AI spend is roughly a tenth of one year of the machines being built to serve it. Some of that gap is legitimate — capex is a multi-year asset, and consumer subscriptions and API developers sit outside the enterprise survey. None of the available data closes it.

2.What they actually buy

Three findings are strong enough to build on, and all three point the same way: the enterprise has stopped experimenting and started purchasing.

They buy rather than build. 76% of enterprise AI use cases were purchased rather than built internally in 2025, against 53% purchased in 2024 [s] — a one-year reversal. MIT's independent survey agrees on the direction and the mechanism: purchased tools and vendor partnerships succeeded about 67% of the time, roughly three times the rate of internal builds [s].

The money is no longer discretionary. The share of enterprise LLM spending funded from innovation budgets fell from 25% in 2024 to 7% in 2025 [s]. This is the single strongest rebuttal to the pilot-stall thesis, and the sample is 100 CIOs across 15 industries — small for the weight it carries, from a venture firm with the same incentive the site flags on Menlo.

Adoption is broad and shallow. 55.0% of businesses on Ramp's platform had paid AI adoption in June 2026 [s], up from its own January 2026 estimate. That is primary transaction data across tens of thousands of businesses, which makes it the best-evidenced number on this page — and Ramp's own census-comparable estimate is far lower, because its customer base skews young and technology-forward. Use it as a leading indicator, not a national rate. Meanwhile the median AI spend per employee was $10.59 in the June 2026 index period [s], on a distribution the source describes as extremely skewed.

forecast · a16z (survey of 100+ CIOs, 2025)Enterprise buyers expected average LLM spend to grow roughly 75% over the following year [f]. This is a stated expectation, not realized spend.

3.The contradiction: 5% or 47%

Menlo reports that 47% of enterprise AI deals reached production in 2025, against 25% for traditional SaaS [s]. MIT NANDA reports that about 5% of custom enterprise generative AI tool efforts were successfully implemented [s].

The corpus previously defused this by saying MIT measured P&L outcome while Menlo measured deployment. That explanation does not survive the primary source. MIT's own section is titled "the pilot-to-production chasm" and its sentence is that 5% of custom enterprise AI tools reach production. Nominally, these are the same metric, nine times apart. Three real axes separate them, and none is a rounding difference:

  • Population. MIT counts custom internal builds. Menlo counts purchased deals. Both studies independently find purchased tools succeed several times more often than internal ones [s][s], so the two numbers are partly measuring the two ends of that same spread.
  • Success bar. Menlo never defines "go to production" anywhere in its report. MIT defines success as a tool causing "a marked and sustained productivity and/or P&L impact" — a much higher bar, and one that would fail plenty of shipped software.
  • Incentive. Menlo is a venture firm and an Anthropic investor. MIT NANDA's report is dated July 2025, self-labelled Preliminary Findings, and self-limits to being "directionally accurate based on individual interviews rather than official company reporting."

MIT's methodology also deserves stating accurately, because it has been inflated in circulation: per the report itself, a review of over 300 publicly disclosed AI initiatives, structured interviews with representatives of 52 organizations, and survey responses from 153 senior leaders collected at four industry conferences. Not 150 interviews and a 350-person employee survey, which is what the corpus previously recorded. And the phrase "rapid revenue acceleration" attached to the 5% figure in most coverage is Fortune's, not MIT's.

The honest synthesis: neither number is a measurement of enterprise AI success. One is a purchased-deal deployment rate with an undefined bar, the other is a custom-build P&L rate from a preliminary conference survey. They are consistent with a world where buying works, building mostly does not, and nobody has yet measured the return on either.

4.Where adoption is genuinely deep

One use case is not in dispute. 50% of developers used AI coding tools daily in 2025 [s], and coding is where the application layer's revenue is concentrated. Everything else is thinner than the discourse suggests: only 16% of enterprise AI deployments qualified as true agents in Menlo's 2025 survey [s], with prompt design still the dominant customization technique. Agentic architectures are a minority of production deployments.

Vendor share is the one place where survey and transaction data can be checked against each other, and they agree. Menlo puts Anthropic at 40% of enterprise LLM spend against 27% for OpenAI and 21% for Google [e] — from an Anthropic investor, so flagged. Ramp's independent card data puts 42.4% of AI-spending businesses paying Anthropic against 39.5% paying OpenAI [s]. These measure different things — spend share versus a headcount of paying businesses — and the vendor gap is far narrower in Ramp's data than in Menlo's. But the ordering survives an independent test, which almost nothing else on this page does.

5.Failure modes

The bar gets defined. Menlo's 47% [s] rests on an undefined "production". The first credible, defined measurement of enterprise AI ROI is the single event most likely to reprice this layer, in either direction.

Shallow spend does not deepen. $10.59 of median AI spend per employee per month [s] is a rounding error in an IT budget. Broad adoption at that depth supports a large number of small vendors, not $376.1bn of annual capex [s].

Buy-versus-build reverses again. The 53%-to-76% swing toward purchasing [s] is one year old and moved fast in one direction. It can move back if model capability makes internal builds cheap, and the application layer's revenue is the collateral.

The measurement apparatus is the risk. Two venture surveys and one payments processor set the consensus for a layer that justifies trillions of forward commitments. Ramp's own selection bias, disclosed by Ramp [s], is the most honest caveat in the corpus and it applies to the most-cited adoption number in the market.

6.Open questions

  • Has anyone measured the return? No. Both flagship studies measure deployment or self-reported impact [s][s]. There is no audited enterprise AI ROI figure in the corpus, from any source, at any date.
  • Does the 75% expected spend growth materialize? It is a buyer expectation from a 2025 survey [f], and the cleanest falsifiable test on this page: Menlo's 2026 model will either show it or it will not.
  • Is Ramp's 55% a leading indicator or a ceiling? [s] The gap between it and Ramp's own census-comparable estimate is the whole question, and Ramp is the only party who can close it.
  • Where does the money actually create value? MIT's finding that over half of genAI budgets go to sales and marketing while measured returns concentrate in back-office automation [s] implies a large misallocation, on a preliminary report's evidence.
  • What is the churn rate? Nowhere in this corpus. Adoption counts are published; renewals are not. For a layer whose entire thesis is recurring budget, that is the gap that matters most.

◆The verdict

evidence-led verdict · updated 2026-07-25

Overbuilt — Enterprise genAI spend tripled to $37bn and moved into core IT budgets. It is still a tenth of one year of big-four capex, and the median employee's AI bill is under eleven dollars a month.

the counter · a16z's 2025 enterprise survey — 100 CIOs, from a firm with portfolio exposure to the answerThe share of enterprise LLM spend funded from innovation budgets fell from 25% to 7% in one year. The money left the experiment column and entered recurring IT. That is not the shape of a pilot bubble.
