Grid & Interconnect

Transformers, HV switchgear, ISO queues, PPAs, utilities

The bottleneck inverted. For twenty years the queue was full of generators waiting to sell power. Now it is full of buyers waiting to consume it.

That is the fact that reorganises this layer. ERCOT is tracking 438 GW of large-load interconnection requests, nearly 90% of them data centers [s] — 5.1 times its all-time peak demand of 85,508 MW, set in August 2023 [s], both figures stated in the same ERCOT document. Its generation queue, by comparison, holds 432 GW [s]. Two queues of almost identical size, pointing in opposite directions, and only one of them used to exist.

1.What this layer is

Between a power plant and a rack sit three things nobody photographs: a large power transformer, a high-voltage substation, and a position in a queue.

The first two are steel and copper with multi-year lead times. Large power transformer waits often exceed 24 months, with prices up roughly 77% since 2019 [e] — and that is the weakest-sourced number on this page, a vendor blog relaying paywalled Wood Mackenzie research, so treat it as directional only.

The third is the actual product. A data center does not buy electricity from this layer. It buys the right to connect, and then a bill for the network upgrades its connection triggers. Everything else on this page follows from that.

The physical build is not keeping pace with the paperwork. The US built 888 miles of new transmission at 345 kV and above in 2024, against 322 miles in 2023 and roughly 5,000 miles per year indicated as necessary by the DOE's 2024 National Transmission Planning Study [s] — and that DOE benchmark did not incorporate the load growth that has since been forecast, so it understates the need. NERC reports 41,000 miles above 100 kV under construction or in development over ten years, up from 28,275 miles a year earlier [s]; the increase is concentrated in planning phases, and miles actually under construction have not risen substantially.

PJM's capacity auction for the 2028–2029 delivery year cleared at its $325 per MW-day price cap on July 15 2026, totalling $16.4bn [s]. Two things travel with that number. PJM states the $16.4bn total was unchanged from the prior auction — the cap is binding, but the auction total was flat year over year, not escalating. And PJM states it does not equate to a total cost to load, because load hedged through self-supply or bilateral contracts is not exposed to clearing prices. What the cap is hiding: PJM's uncapped simulation clears far higher, which is the more honest measure of scarcity.

The auction cleared 6.8 GW below its 20% installed reserve margin target and attracted 525 MW of new supply [s]. Read those two figures together. At the maximum price the market is allowed to pay, half a gigawatt of new capacity showed up. Price is not the constraint. Delivery is.

Who pays. PJM's independent market monitor attributes $29.4bn of the $63.6bn in total capacity charges across the last four auctions to data centers, including $6.3bn of the $16.4bn in July 2026 [e] — roughly 46% of the four-auction total, on the market monitor's own counterfactual attribution across the base residual auctions for delivery years 2025/26 through 2028/29. It is a model, and the monitor says so. It is also the most precise measurement of cost socialisation anywhere in this corpus, made by the entity paid to be adversarial to PJM.

Wholesale costs moved with it: PJM wholesale power costs rose 54% in one year per Monitoring Analytics in March 2026 [s]. That is a headline-level capture on a specific window, and the monitor's later deck restates the increase on a different window — the window matters more than the number.

3.The players

PJM is the stress case, because it stopped taking applications. Its interconnection queue was effectively closed to new entrants from 2022 until April 2026 [s], on a pre-reform backlog it could not clear. It studied approximately 160 GW between the July 2023 reform launch and September 2025, with roughly 46 GW left to complete by end-2026 [s]. Transition Cycle 1 closed in September 2025 with 130 requests totalling 17.4 GW [s] — a rounding error against what arrived next.

Then the queue reopened, and the composition flipped. Cycle 1 drew 811 projects totalling 220 GW of nameplate capacity [s], led by gas at 106 GW [s] — a reversal from the renewables-dominated queues of 2020 to 2024. PJM publishes the caveat that matters and it is usually dropped: the figures represent maximum potential output, and based on historical performance only a percentage of applicants will sign interconnection agreements. The corpus can put a number on that percentage. 24% of PJM's 2020 entry cohort reached an interconnection agreement or became operational [s] — a generation cohort, but the only realisation prior anyone has. A 220 GW queue is not a 220 GW pipeline.

ERCOT is building the process the load queue exposed it as lacking. The PUCT approved its initial large-load batch-study process on June 18 2026, covering projects of 75 MW or larger [s]. ERCOT attaches its own attrition caveat to the queue: Batch Zero developers face a commitment deadline to post financial security and prove site control before their requests survive. Its queue grew from 63 GW at end-2024 to 226 GW in November 2025 [s] before reaching 438 GW.

MISO is the quieter version: 1,705 projects and 312 GW in the queue as of early 2025 [s], with a Definitive Planning Phase averaging three to four years against a stated goal of a one-year process by the 2028 cycle [e].

FERC is the referee, and it has started intervening on the load side rather than the generation side. In December 2025 it ordered PJM to create four transmission service options for co-located large loads, finding the existing tariff unjust and unreasonable [s]. In June 2026 it issued show-cause orders to all six FERC-jurisdictional RTOs and ISOs, requiring them to justify or revise large-load interconnection rules within 60 days [s] — its proposed elements include a standard large-load threshold, hourly forecast and telemetry requirements, and pro forma agreements making large-load customers bear network-upgrade costs. The regulator is trying to un-socialise the bill.

The hyperscalers are increasingly refusing to wait. Microsoft announced a 2 GW data center in Pecos, Texas in June 2026 with the company funding the energy infrastructure itself [s]. FERC's earlier rejection of the amended Talen–Amazon interconnection agreement for a 480 MW behind-the-meter expansion at Susquehanna [s] is the ruling that pushed co-location deals front-of-meter and made this the default shape.

4.Concentration & chokepoints

Load growth is not spread across the country. Six regions account for over 80% of forecast US five-year load growth: ERCOT 53 GW, PJM 30 GW, SPP 24 GW, MISO 16 GW, Georgia Power 8 GW, CAISO 7 GW [f]. PJM's share is driven by Northern Virginia; ERCOT's by data centers, industry and oil and gas.

The chokepoint is therefore geographic and administrative at once. Two RTOs decide most of the outcome, one of them has just reopened a queue after four years [s], and the other is inventing its large-load process in public [s].

A second chokepoint is the load shape. The US power system operates at about a 60% load factor as of 2025, up from 58% three years earlier, and is forecast to reach 66% in 2030 [e]. Data centers add energy faster than they add peak: they run flat, and utilities are now planning around load factors far above the system average. A flatter system is more efficient and also less forgiving — there is less slack to absorb the next large customer.

5.Lead times & capacity

Generation interconnection waits are the number everyone quotes, and the word "generation" has to stay attached to it. Average generation interconnection wait time in PJM was 40 months as of May 2026, against a federal target of 8 to 11 months [e]. Active generation projects inside the data-center load-growth zones of PJM and ERCOT were waiting 36 to 48 months [e]. These are not load-queue waits — the load queue is new enough that ERCOT is still designing the process that will produce its first timelines.

PJM still has roughly 46 GW of studies to finish by the end of 2026 before its reformed process is caught up [s]. Against a data center shell that can be built in eighteen months, the study calendar is the schedule.

What happens if demand doubles is already partly on the record, as a forecast, from the reliability regulator.

forecast · NERC (2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment, published January 2026)US summer peak demand growth of approximately 224 GW over ten years, nearly 70% above the prior year's forecast [f]. And 13 of 23 assessment areas face resource adequacy challenges over that period [f]. The wording matters: NERC is explicit that Elevated-Risk areas meet resource adequacy criteria and fall short only under extreme conditions, and that only High-Risk areas exceed shortfall criteria. "Thirteen areas face shortfalls" overstates what the assessment says.
forecast · Ascend AnalyticsA potential PJM capacity shortage reaching 15 GW by 2030 as load additions outpace new generation [f] — consistent with an auction whose reserve-margin shortfall is already 6.8 GW while attracting 525 MW of new supply [s].

PJM's answer is procurement rather than construction: in April 2026 it proposed adding 14.9 GW through bilateral contracts and a central backstop matching large loads with generation sellers [s].

6.2019 → today

2019. Interconnection is a specialist concern. Utility five-year peak load forecasts are essentially flat; the industry's planning problem is retirement, not growth.

2022. Aggregate US utility five-year summer peak load growth forecasts filed with FERC total 24 GW [f]. PJM's queue closes to new entrants [s]. Data centers are 4.4% of national electricity [s] and nobody outside the sector is counting.

2026. Those same FERC filings total 166 GW by 2030 — a six-fold increase in three years [f], of which roughly 90 GW, about 55%, is linked to data centers [e]. ERCOT's large-load queue goes from 63 GW to 438 GW inside eighteen months [s][s]. FERC opens proceedings against all six RTOs [s]. NERC issues its highest-tier alert on computational load [s].

The steelman, and its counter — both from the same report. Grid Strategies estimates the data center portion of utility load forecasts is overstated by roughly 25 GW of that ~90 GW, on the view that data center growth is unlikely to require much more than 65 GW through 2030 [e][e]. The drivers it names are duplicate requests, unrealistically high assumed load factors, and timing optimism. That is the strongest available argument that this page is describing a paperwork bubble. But the same report notes that utility forecasts of 2022–2025 growth were revised up, not down, with actual load growth exceeding what had been forecast, and that PJM is evaluating forecast adjustments that would add to its 2030 load forecast. Utilities and RTOs continue to plan against the full 166 GW. Whoever quotes the overstatement finding without the revision finding is quoting half a paper.

7.Commoditization pressure

The opposite is happening here, and it is the most under-appreciated fact on this page.

Grid interconnection used to be a regulated, essentially free service with a queue that moved. It is becoming a priced, rationed, contractually complex asset — the exact reverse of commoditization. The evidence is structural, not rhetorical: a capacity auction clearing at its administrative cap [s], four distinct new transmission service classes for co-located load invented by regulatory order [s], a batch-study process with financial-security postings and site-control proof for anything at 75 MW or above [s], and six simultaneous FERC proceedings to define a threshold above which a customer becomes a different regulatory category entirely [s].

The historical analogue is not electricity. It is airport slots: a public good that became a tradeable, scarce, litigated asset the moment demand outran runway.

The one genuine commoditization pressure runs the other way, through flexibility. Google had signed 1 GW of data center demand response with five utilities as of March 2026 [s]. If interruptible load becomes a standard product, the scarce asset is un-scarced by contract rather than by construction.

8.Failure modes

The reliability event. NERC issued a Level 3 Essential Actions alert — its highest tier — on May 4 2026 on computational load, after observing customer-initiated large load reductions and oscillations that occur within seconds; two data center load-reduction events exceeded 1,000 MW during 2025, the largest 1,800 MW [s]. Four qualifiers travel with that, and the site prints all four. The reductions occur in seconds, not sub-second. There were two such events in 2025, not repeated ones — the largest caused by a transmission fault in the Eastern Interconnection. The alert is explicitly non-binding: it creates no mandatory obligation and carries no penalties. And NERC's "computational load" category includes cryptocurrency mining as well as AI data centers, so part of the event record is not AI at all. What survives all four qualifiers is still serious: the regulator's highest tier of alert, on a load type that can drop a gigawatt in seconds.

Cost socialisation becomes political. $29.4bn of $63.6bn in capacity charges attributed to data centers [e] is a number that appears on retail bills. The market monitor has proposed separate capacity auctions for data centers. Blast radius: state legislatures, and the siting permission every layer above this one depends on.

Capacity shortfall in the largest market. Ascend Analytics' 15 GW PJM shortage by 2030 [f], against 525 MW of new supply attracted at the price cap [s], is a mechanism, not a scenario: prices cannot rise further, so the adjustment has to come through curtailment, backstop procurement or refused interconnections.

The queue turns out to be phantom. If the 24% realisation rate of PJM's 2020 cohort [s] is anywhere near the right prior for today's 220 GW [s], every transmission investment sized against the queue is oversized — and the utilities making those investments recover them from ratepayers regardless.

9.Open questions

  • What is the realisation rate on a load queue? The corpus has one for generation — 24% for PJM's 2020 cohort [s] — and none for large load, because ERCOT's process is a month old [s]. Every argument about the 438 GW rests on a number nobody has.
  • Overstated by 25 GW, or revised up again? Grid Strategies argues both in one report [e]. The 2027 FERC 714 filings settle it.
  • Does the cap hold? The 2028/29 auction cleared at its $325/MW-day cap, as the prior auction cleared at its own [s]. If the cap is raised, the total stops being flat; if it holds, the shortfall has to resolve physically rather than through price.
  • How much load is genuinely interruptible? Google's 1 GW [s] comes with Google's own caveat that flexibility is limited and location-specific. Nobody has published a system-wide number.
  • How much of NERC's computational-load record is AI? The alert's category includes crypto mining [s], and the corpus cannot split the event record between the two.
  • What are transformer lead times, really? The best-sourced figure available is a vendor blog relaying paywalled research [e]. For a component that gates every interconnection, that is not good enough, and the site says so rather than printing the more dramatic numbers circulating.
