Cloud & Neoclouds

Hyperscalers, CoreWeave-class, capex, depreciation, SPVs

Every argument about whether AI is a bubble eventually lands here. Not on the chips, and not on the models — on the depreciation schedule of the machines in between.

This is the layer where the money becomes an asset, and where an assumption about how long that asset lasts decides whether the industry is profitable. Change the number from five years to six and roughly seven billion dollars of operating income appears [e]. Nobody has to lie for this to go wrong. They only have to be optimistic about how long a GPU stays useful.

1.What this layer is

Two businesses wearing one name.

The hyperscalers — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta — own the land, the power contracts, the buildings and the machines, and rent compute as one line in a much larger software business. The neoclouds — CoreWeave, Nebius, Crusoe, Lambda and several hundred smaller entrants [e] — do only this. They buy GPUs with borrowed money and rent them out, which means they have no other revenue to hide a bad year in.

The physical anchor: a rack of eight-GPU servers, drawing more power than a suburban street, sitting in a shell that took three years to permit and energise. The hyperscaler treats it as a depreciating asset on a balance sheet full of other things. The neocloud treats it as collateral.

Calendar 2025 combined capital expenditure across the big four was $376.1bn in cash, or $406.4bn including finance leases [s]. Q1 2026 alone was $129.8bn, up 80% year over year [s].

The ratio that matters: over the trailing four quarters to March 2026, that capex ran at approximately 2.9 times the $149.2bn of depreciation and finance-lease amortization behind it [e]. Two qualifiers travel with that number and the site will not print it without them. The denominator excludes intangible amortization — include it and the ratio is 2.78. And Amazon is 47% of the denominator on a caption that bundles roughly $16–19bn a year of capitalised video content; strip the non-PP&E items and the ratio goes above three. The strongest version of the depreciation argument is the one its proponents have not been making.

Cash conversion is where it bites. Amazon's trailing-twelve-month capex equalled 102% of operating cash flow [s]. The business is now spending slightly more on machines than it generates from operations.

3.The players

The hyperscalers guided roughly $650–725bn for 2026 — Meta at $125–145bn [s], Alphabet $175–185bn and rising [s], Amazon around $200bn [s], Microsoft tracking near $190bn [e]. Treat the aggregate as a spread, not a number: it comes from three different forecasters, none of it is guidance in the strict sense [e], and Q1 actuals annualise to about $519bn, so every version of it requires a steep intra-year ramp.

CoreWeave is the layer's stress test, because it runs the same business without the cover. Q1 2026 revenue $2.078bn, up 112% [s]. Adjusted EBITDA $1.157bn at a 56% margin — and a GAAP net loss of $740m in the same quarter [s]. Both numbers are true at once, and the bridge between them is $536m of quarterly net interest [s] against $24.9bn of debt on $4.8bn of equity [s]. Backlog is $99.4bn [s]; capex is outrunning operating cash flow roughly five-to-two [s].

Nebius is the same shape earlier: $399m of quarterly revenue, up 684% [s], against contracts of $17.4bn with Microsoft [s] and about $27bn with Meta [s], and 2026 capex guidance of $20–25bn on $3.0–3.4bn of revenue [f].

Oracle has quietly become a neocloud with an enterprise software business attached: $55.7bn of FY2026 capex against guidance of about $50bn [s], and RPO of $523bn [s].

4.Concentration & chokepoints

The chokepoint here is not technical. It is counterparty concentration, and it points at one customer.

OpenAI has contracted roughly $300bn with Oracle [s], an incremental $250bn with Azure [s], and up to $11.9bn with CoreWeave [s] — against a company whose annualized revenue run rate exited 2025 above $20bn [e]. Sam Altman has put total commitments at about $1.4 trillion across roughly 30 GW and eight years [o].

Two honesty notes on that comparison, because it is the most-abused number in the discourse. The $1.4tn is Altman's own attributed figure, not a sum of the contracts above — those account for $588bn of it , and the remainder is not publicly itemised. And it spans about eight years, so setting it against a single year of revenue compares unlike things. The real observation is narrower and still striking: the layer's growth is underwritten by forward commitments from a small number of counterparties, at least one of which is spending far ahead of its income.

CoreWeave's own concentration was 62% Microsoft as recently as 2024 [s]; the Meta agreement at $21bn [s] diversifies the name at the top of the list without changing the shape of the risk.

5.Lead times & capacity

Demand is not the constraint and has not been for two years. Microsoft reported $633bn of remaining performance obligations, up from $398bn six months earlier, while stating plainly that demand continues to exceed supply [s]. Alphabet's total RPO reached $519.5bn as of June 2026, against $108.2bn a year earlier — it roughly doubled in a single quarter [s].

Supply is the constraint, and it is upstream of this layer: power, shells, and volume GPU lead times reported at 36–52 weeks [e]. If demand doubled tomorrow the answer would be set by the grid queue and the turbine order book, not by anything a cloud provider controls.

6.2019 → today

2019. Cloud is a software-margin business. Capex tracks revenue. Servers depreciate over three or four years and nobody outside accounting cares.

2022–23. The useful-life extensions arrive, and they arrive everywhere. Microsoft goes from four years to six [s]. Alphabet goes to six, worth about $3.4bn of depreciation [s]. Amazon follows to six, worth about $3.2bn [s]. Each is defensible alone. Together they lengthen the industry's assumed asset life just as the assets start changing faster.

2025–26. The consensus breaks in public. In January 2025 Meta extends to 5.5 years [s] — and Amazon shortens a subset of servers from six years back to five, taking $889m of extra depreciation and $677m off net income in nine months [s]. Two companies with the same machines, the same customers and the same quarter, moving in opposite directions.

Meta's own numbers show what the extension is fighting: depreciation on property and equipment still rose from about $11.0bn in 2023 to $18.0bn in 2025, despite the longer life [s].

7.Commoditization pressure

Renting a GPU is commoditizing. Renting a platform is not, and the price data separates them cleanly.

H100 on-demand pricing fell 64–75% from its 2024 peak, to $1.80–3.50 an hour [e], having once exceeded $12 at list [e]. But one-year reserved contract pricing bottomed at $1.70 in October 2025 and rebounded about 38% to $2.35 by March 2026 [s] — providers sold out, and little Hopper capacity came off contract. And hyperscaler on-demand pricing still runs three-to-five times marketplace rates [s].

So the deflation is real, generation-tiered, and not monotonic. The useful evidence against "GPUs are a commodity that depreciates to zero in three years" is Azure retiring V100s roughly 7.5 years after launch [s]. Newer silicon is not following the same curve — B200 rentals were $4.50–7.00 an hour in Q2 2026 with marketplace rates rising month over month [s].

8.Failure modes

The depreciation reset. If useful lives compress back toward four years, the added depreciation lands directly on operating income at roughly $7bn per year per year of change [e]. Amazon has already moved one subset in that direction. Blast radius: earnings across the big four, and every valuation resting on them.

The leveraged neocloud. CoreWeave shows the mechanism — 56% EBITDA margins and a GAAP loss, separated by interest on $24.9bn of debt. A refinancing window that closes, or a large counterparty that renegotiates, converts a growth story into a solvency one. The financing structure is now explicit about this: the $8.5bn facility closed in March 2026 sits in a bankruptcy-remote subsidiary, secured on substantially all of that subsidiary's assets [s]. The GPUs are the lien.

Off-balance-sheet contagion. Meta and Blue Owl's Richland Parish venture commits the parties to pro-rata shares of roughly $27bn in development costs [s], structured so most of it does not consolidate. Morgan Stanley projects about $1.5tn of data-center debt financing need through 2028, roughly $800bn from private credit [f]. That is where a shock would be hardest to see.

Counterparty failure. One customer's inability to pay its compute bill would reprice the backlog of everyone who sold to it.

9.Open questions

  • Which useful life is right? Nobody knows, including the operators — that is what January 2025 demonstrated. The V100 lasting 7.5 years [s] and a B200 rental market that is still tightening both argue for longer lives; the pace of architectural change argues for shorter.
  • Does the 2026 ramp happen? Q1 actuals [s] annualise to ~$519bn against the $650–725bn forecast range [e]. The gap resolves by December, one way or the other, and it is the cleanest falsifiable test on this page.
  • Is contracted backlog the same as revenue? Alphabet's RPO quadrupled in a year. RPO measures obligations, not cash, and the site has no data on cancellation or renegotiation terms.
  • Who holds the residual risk in the SPVs? The disclosures give exposure ceilings, not probabilities. Meta's own filing puts maximum exposure to loss at $45.95bn against $1.83bn of carried equity in one venture [s].
  • What happens to the neoclouds if hyperscaler capex flattens? They are the marginal buyer of GPUs and the marginal seller of capacity. A pause upstream reaches them first.
